In the last week of December 2025, a household in Medellín was doing everything the guides tell you to do.

They had read the regulation. Not a summary of the regulation, the regulation. Colombia's visa framework had been published as Resolución 5477 of 2022, and it had arrived with ninety days between publication and entry into force, which is a civilised interval by any standard. They had found the article that set the income requirement for their visa category. They had understood that the requirement was not stated as a peso figure but as a multiple of the statutory monthly minimum wage. They had done the arithmetic. They had a number, and the number worked, and they had built a life around it.

On 29 December the government published Decretos 1469 and 1470 of 2025. The statutory minimum wage rose twenty-three percent. The decrees took effect on 1 January.

Two days.

Nothing in the regulation the household had studied changed. Not a word of it. The article they had read still said exactly what it had said in 2022, and the multiple it specified was the same multiple. What moved was the number the multiple points at, and it moved by twenty-three percent, in a decree published between Christmas and New Year, binding three days later.

I want to be precise about what happened there, because the ordinary way of describing it is wrong. This household was not caught out by a rule change. There was no rule change. They were not careless, and they were not badly advised, and they had not skipped a step. They were caught out by the interval. The information they needed existed. It was published. It was public. It simply was not published early enough for anyone to do anything with it.

That interval has a name in this book, and it is the thing the rest of this book is about.

## What notice is

**Notice is the interval between the date an instrument is published and the date it binds.**

That is the whole definition. It is deliberately narrow, and the narrowness is what makes it useful.

Notice is not the same thing as fairness. A government can give you two years of notice on a policy that ruins you, and one day of notice on a policy that costs you nothing. Notice says nothing about whether a change is good, justified, well designed, or well intentioned. It is not a political measure and it does not lean in any direction. Both parties in every country in this ledger have signed decrees with short fuses.

Notice is not the same thing as predictability in the loose sense either. A household might feel that a country is predictable because they have been there five years and nothing has gone wrong. Notice is not a feeling. It is a number of days, and you can count it, and two people counting it independently will get the same answer.

And notice is not the same thing as the size of a change. This turns out to be the most important thing about it, and it is the first of the four findings below.

What notice measures is the one thing that determines whether information can be acted on: how long you had. A change you learn about with 182 days remaining is a problem you can solve. You can move money, restructure income, file early, get an appointment, take advice, or decide it is time to go somewhere else. The same change with two days remaining is not a problem you can solve. It is an outcome you absorb.

Chapter 5 made the argument that predictability, not control, is the part of this that can be manufactured. Notice is what predictability looks like when you write it down as a number. It is the operational form of the thing the evidence says actually matters.

## Why nobody has counted this

Here is what I find genuinely strange. This is the single most consequential variable in cross-border life, it requires no model, no proprietary data, no access, and no statistical training to measure, and as far as I have been able to establish, nobody publishes it.

The relocation industry does not publish it, and chapter 14 explains why in economic terms rather than moral ones. Briefly: a firm paid a commission when a household moves has no commercial reason to publish a measure of how fast the ground moves under the households it has already placed.

The professional advisory world does not publish it either, but for a different and more forgivable reason. A cross-border tax practitioner is paid to get a specific file right under the rules as they currently stand. Tracking the rate at which the rules arrive is not billable to any client, and it is nobody's job.

Academic work on regulatory notice exists in administrative law, but it studies notice-and-comment procedure in single jurisdictions, which is a different question. It asks whether a rulemaking process gave affected parties a chance to be heard. It does not ask how many days a household in Medellín had between finding out and being bound.

So the count below is, as far as I know, the first of its kind. That is a claim about the state of a small literature rather than a claim about my own cleverness. The measurement is trivial. Anyone could have done it. The reason to do it is that once the intervals are in one table, they say something that none of them says alone.

## The ledger

Nine instruments, seven jurisdictions, published between 2022 and 2026. Each row carries the date the instrument was published, the date it took effect, the interval between them in days, and the mechanism that produced it.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spain | RD 1155/2024, immigration regulation | 20 Nov 2024 | 20 May 2025 | 182 days | Legislative, scheduled |
| Colombia | Resolución 5477/2022, visa framework | 22 Jul 2022 | 21 Oct 2022 | 90 days | Ministerial, statutory delay |
| Thailand | Paw. 161/2566, tax reinterpretation | 15 Sep 2023 | 1 Jan 2024 | about 107 days | Departmental instruction |
| United States | 2026 COLA | 24 Oct 2025 | Dec 2025 | about 38 days | Statutory formula, input delayed |
| Mexico | UMA 2026 | 8 Jan 2026 | 1 Feb 2026 | 24 days | Published indexation formula |
| Colombia | Decretos 1469 and 1470 of 2025, wage plus 23 percent | 29 Dec 2025 | 1 Jan 2026 | 2 days | Executive decree |
| Portugal | DL 139/2025, wage plus 5.7 percent | 29 Dec 2025 | 1 Jan 2026 | 3 days | Executive decree |
| Spain | RD 316/2026, amending RD 1155/2024 | 15 Apr 2026 | 16 Apr 2026 | 1 day | Executive decree |
| Italy | Elective residency income floor | none | none | no published figure | Consular discretion |

Read the notice column from top to bottom. One hundred and eighty-two days. Ninety. About a hundred and seven. About thirty-eight. Twenty-four. Two. Three. One. And then a row where the question cannot be asked.

Four findings come out of that column. None of them requires a model, and none of them requires you to take my word for anything, because every row is a public document with a publication date printed on it.

## Finding one. Notice is uncorrelated with the size of the change

Colombia's statutory minimum wage rose twenty-three percent in a single step, with two days of notice.

Spain's six-month runway, the longest interval in the ledger, accompanied a procedural reform with no comparable financial consequence for a household.

Put those two rows beside each other and the intuitive model breaks. The intuitive model says that big changes come with long warnings and small changes come with short ones, because that is what a reasonable institution would do. It is not what happens. The largest financial move in the ledger came with the second shortest interval, and the longest interval came attached to a change that mostly reorganised paperwork.

This has a direct consequence for how a household should behave, and it is not the consequence people expect. It means you cannot use the size of a potential change to decide how closely to watch for it. The magnitude of the exposure tells you nothing about how much warning you will get. A household that reasons "this would be a huge change, so surely they would tell us well in advance" has made an inference the data does not support.

The two variables are separate. You have to track them separately.

## Finding two. Notice is collapsing within jurisdictions, not only across them

This is the finding that persuades me, and it rests on two rows.

On 20 November 2024, Spain published Royal Decree 1155/2024, a substantial immigration regulation. It took effect on 20 May 2025. One hundred and eighty-two days.

On 15 April 2026, Spain published Royal Decree 316/2026, which amends Royal Decree 1155/2024. It took effect on 16 April 2026. One day.

Same country. Same legal system. Same ministry. Same underlying regulation, one instrument amending the other. Eighteen months apart.

One hundred and eighty-two days, then one.

I want to explain why I keep returning to this pair, because on its own it might look like a single anomaly rather than a trend, and one anomaly is not a finding.

The problem with comparing notice across countries is that you are never comparing like with like. Spain and Colombia have different legal traditions, different constitutional constraints on executive action, different administrative capacity, different politics, different everything. If Spanish notice is long and Colombian notice is short, the honest reading is that these are two different systems and you have learned very little.

The Spain pair removes that objection entirely. It holds the legal system constant. The constitution did not change between November 2024 and April 2026. The administrative capacity did not change. The tools available to the ministry did not change. The ministry had the option of a long runway, because it had used one on the parent regulation eighteen months earlier, and it chose a one-day runway instead.

That is not a difference between legal systems. That is the same legal system, doing the same kind of thing, with a different interval. Whatever changed, it was not the constraint. It was the practice.

I hold this finding at roughly seventy percent, in the language chapter 18 will teach you to use, and I will say exactly what would move it. Two rows is two rows. If a systematic extension of this ledger to thirty rows across fifteen jurisdictions finds that within-jurisdiction intervals are stable and the Spain pair is an outlier, this finding fails and I will publish that it failed. That extension is the first research task listed in this book's own source map, and the reason it is first is that it is the task most likely to embarrass me.

## Finding three. Indexation moves the surprise from the rule to the index

Come back to the household in Medellín, because their situation is the general case rather than a hard-luck story.

Colombia's visa framework was published with ninety days of notice, which is a real interval. Nothing about that regulation was rushed or hidden. And the regulation states its income threshold not as a fixed peso amount but as a multiple of the statutory minimum wage.

That construction is defensible. Arguably it is the responsible way to write the rule. An indexed threshold does not silently erode with inflation, it does not require the ministry to reopen the regulation every year, and it keeps the requirement in a stable relationship to local wages. Every incentive pushes toward writing rules this way, and more jurisdictions are doing it, not fewer.

But look at what indexation does to notice.

**The rule gives you the multiple. The gazette gives you the multiplicand.**

The multiple is stable, published, and available years in advance. You can read it whenever you like. The multiplicand is set separately, by a different instrument, through a different process, on a different calendar, and in Colombia's case it arrives in the last week of December and binds on 1 January.

So a household can study the regulation as carefully as anyone has ever studied a regulation, understand it completely, and still not know what their income requirement will be, because the regulation does not contain that information. It contains half of it. The other half is published two days before it binds.

This is why the ninety-day figure in the second row of the ledger is misleading if you read it alone, and it is why I have come to think notice has to be measured on the binding number rather than on the instrument. The regulation had ninety days of notice. The threshold had two.

The same structure appears in the Mexico row from a different angle. The UMA is a published indexation formula, which sounds like the most transparent arrangement possible, and in one sense it is. The formula is public. Anyone can read it. But UMA 2026 was published on 8 January and took effect on 1 February, which is twenty-four days, and a household whose obligations are denominated in UMA finds out what those obligations are three and a half weeks before they apply.

A published formula is better than a decree. Chapter 9 gives the rigorous reason why, and it comes from North: a formula is a commitment device and a decree is not, which means an indexed threshold is worth more than a lower number set by discretion. I want to be careful not to overstate the problem here. Indexation is a genuine improvement in the durability of a rule.

What indexation is not is an improvement in notice. It relocates the surprise. It moves it from a place you are watching, the regulation, to a place you are probably not watching, the gazette, in the last week of December.

## Finding four. Discretion produces the limiting case

The Italy row is the one that breaks the format, and it belongs in the ledger precisely because it does.

Italy operates an elective residency visa with an income requirement. It publishes no figure for that requirement. There is no number in an instrument to point at, and the authorities reserve full discretion to require more than any figure circulating informally.

So there is no interval to measure. Publication never happens, which means the gap between publication and binding is not long or short, it is undefined. You cannot compute it, and no amount of research effort produces it, because the quantity does not exist.

Chapter 7 gives this its formal name and the name is Knight's. A threshold published as a multiple of a gazetted wage figure is a risk. The outcome is unknown, but the distribution is knowable from the history of the index, and knowable distributions can be forecast. A threshold set by discretion with no published formula is an uncertainty in Knight's strict sense, where the distribution itself is unknown, and no analysis converts it into a risk at any price.

That distinction has a hard commercial edge, and this book will apply it to itself. There are things in this field that can be forecast and things that can only be monitored, and any product that does not tell you which is which on the face of it is selling you something it does not have. I can forecast an indexed Colombian threshold. I cannot forecast an Italian consular decision, and neither can anyone else, and the honest response to a reader who asks me to is to say so.

The Italy row is also where this book's second potential original finding sits, and I flag it here rather than claiming it. Different Italian consulates apply different effective minimums to comparable files. Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein gave that phenomenon a rigorous name in 2021, and it is noise: unwanted variance in judgments that should agree. Noise is measurable, and the measurement method is published, and nobody in this market has run it. If it produces a number, it belongs in chapter 7. If it does not, chapter 7 says so.

## What this ledger does not show

Four things, stated here rather than buried, because a book that spends chapter 4 deflating Marmot cannot then oversell its own table.

**Nine rows is a small number.** This is not a sample from which population parameters can be estimated. It is an existence proof and a first count. It establishes that these intervals vary enormously, that the variation is not explained by the size of the change, and that at least one jurisdiction has moved sharply in one direction on one regulation. It does not establish a global trend, and I am not going to write as if it does. The extension to thirty rows is the task that would either support that claim or kill it.

**These rows were not drawn at random.** They came from instruments that mattered to Sovereo readers between 2022 and 2026, which means they are selected for relevance to a particular kind of household. A random sample of all instruments in these jurisdictions would look different, and it would probably show longer average notice, because most instruments do not bind households and are not rushed. What the selection means is that this ledger measures notice on the instruments that reach you, which is the quantity a reader actually needs. It is not a claim about administrative practice in general.

**The mechanism column contains judgments.** Publication dates and effective dates are facts printed on documents. Calling something a departmental instruction rather than a ministerial act is a characterisation, and a specialist in any one of these jurisdictions might characterise it differently. Where the mechanism label is doing analytical work in this chapter, I have tried to make the underlying document visible so a reader can disagree with the label and keep the row.

**There is no counterfactual.** I cannot tell you what notice looked like in these jurisdictions in 1996, because nobody counted it then either. So when I say notice is collapsing, the honest scope of that claim is the Spain pair and the general shape of the column, not a long time series. The book does not have one. It is starting one.

## Why this is not a complaint about government

I want to close the chapter by ruling something out, because the material lends itself to a reading I do not hold and do not want.

Nothing in this ledger requires anyone to have behaved badly. Chapter 9 goes through the incentives in detail, and they are ordinary. A finance ministry under fiscal pressure moves when it can move. A wage decree is timed to a political calendar that has nothing to do with foreign residents, because foreign residents are not who it is for. A statistical agency reschedules a release because its funding process slipped, and the Social Security row in this ledger is exactly that: a fully public statutory formula, an announcement that had landed between 10 and 13 October for four consecutive years, and then a September CPI moved to 24 October. The formula did not change. The benefit did not change. Only the notice changed.

That is the cleanest illustration in the book of what this chapter is about, and it involves no rule change, no bad actor, and no policy decision aimed at anybody. A statistical agency is funded through a process the household has no relationship with, and the household's notice is a byproduct of that process.

So this is not an argument that states are becoming more hostile. It is an argument about a quantity that nobody is responsible for maintaining, that nobody measures, and that is therefore free to move without anyone noticing or intending it. The households in this ledger were not targeted. They were downstream.

Being downstream of a quantity nobody is watching is a describable position, and it is the position this book is written for. You cannot make Colombia publish its wage decree in November. That is outside the dichotomy Epictetus drew nineteen centuries before Marmot found it again in a cohort of civil servants, and chapter 5 has already conceded it.

What you can do is stop being surprised by the last week of December.

That is what the rest of this book teaches, and it is a smaller promise than the ones you will have been offered elsewhere. It is also the only one the evidence supports.

## Stop point

Five minutes, before you continue.

Open a page and write four column headings: instrument, published, in force, interval.

Now fill in one row. Not a hypothetical one. The single instrument that most determines whether you can stay where you are, or go where you intend to go. The income threshold on your visa category. The tax rule that decides your residency. The benefit formula your household budget assumes.

Write down the date it was last published, the date it took effect, and the number of days between them.

If you cannot find the publication date, write "not found" and leave the row open. That is not a failure of the exercise. It is the first real result the exercise produces, and chapter 16 is about what to do with it.

If the instrument states its threshold as a multiple of something, write down what the something is, and when that something is next set. That second date is the one that will actually reach you.

This is row one of your own notice ledger. You will add to it through Part Two, and by chapter 11 you will have a page that tells you, in days, how much warning your life currently runs on.

Almost nobody has ever seen that number for themselves. It is usually smaller than people expect.
