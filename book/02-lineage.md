The column that matters for the book is the last one. A thinker earns a place by doing work in a chapter, not by being admired.

The book owes this literature its subject and none of its method. The Sovereign Individual forecast the dissolution of the nation state under pressure from digital technology and mobile capital. What happened instead is that states became substantially better at tracking, taxing and pricing mobile people, and FATCA, the Common Reporting Standard, beneficial ownership registries and indexed residency thresholds are all instruments of an apparatus that got stronger in exactly the period the book said it would dissolve. That is a reason to score the literature rather than discard it, and it is the reason chapter 20 ends where it does.

## Two structural guarantees

The lineage above is largely a classical liberal reading list. The empirical foundation of the book is Marmot, who chaired a World Health Organization commission on health equity and belongs to no part of that tradition. Two things hold the book together across that gap, and both are argued in the text rather than asserted.

1. **Sen.** What matters is not the resources a person holds but the range of things they are actually able to do and be. Capability rather than the absence of interference. That framework lets autonomy and health appear in the same sentence without borrowing from a tradition the book does not hold. Marmot draws on him explicitly.
2. **Scott and Hayek.** Scott's metis and Hayek's dispersed knowledge are the same finding, reached by two thinkers from opposite ends of the political spectrum working on different problems. That convergence is the proof the method is not partisan, and it is why chapter 8 pairs them deliberately.
