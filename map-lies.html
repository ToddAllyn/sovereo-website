Why the map is wrong, in plain English

You do not need any background in geography. Here is the whole thing, simply.

What is the distortion?

Most world maps use the Mercator projection. A projection is a method for drawing the round Earth on a flat page. This one was made in 1569 to help ships find their way. To keep compass directions straight, it stretches everything as you move away from the equator. The further a country is from the equator, the bigger the map makes it. Near the poles the stretch is huge.

The famous example

Greenland looks about the size of Africa on a standard map. In reality Africa is roughly fourteen times larger. Greenland is far north, so the map makes it look huge. Africa sits across the equator, so it is drawn close to its true size. Drag any country north and it grows on screen. Drag it to the equator and it shrinks back to its true size.

What this tool does

When you move a country, the tool keeps its true size the same and places its real outline at the new spot. Then it draws it on the same Mercator map. So the change you see on screen is the map's error, not the country changing. The area figure stays the same because the country never really changes size.

Why an intelligence site cares

Because the same habit that misreads a map also misreads the world. A country that looks powerful may be smaller than you think. A place you ignored may be larger or better to live in than the map suggests. Helping you see clearly is the whole point of Sovereo.

How this works. The base map and every outline come from the free Natural Earth dataset, through the world-atlas project. No outside map service is used. The map uses the Mercator projection, the same one that gets size wrong on standard world maps. When you move a country, each point of its outline keeps the same real distance and direction from the country's center. That keeps the real area the same, while the Mercator map shows how its size seems to change. We work out area from the outline on a round Earth and show it in square kilometres and square miles.
