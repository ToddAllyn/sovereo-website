The Sovereo Index · Background and Concepts

Where you live shapes how long, how healthy, and how wealthy your life will be.

Most "best places to live" rankings measure lifestyle. The Sovereo Index, our score of every country, measures your chances in life. It is built on 50 years of health research. That research shows the conditions around you shape your health and lifespan as much as anything a doctor does.

The idea

In 1967 the health researcher Michael Marmot began following thousands of British government workers. These are the Whitehall studies. They found something that changed public health: the risk of dying followed a clear social gradient (a step-by-step ladder). The lower a person's rank at work, the higher their risk of dying, at every step. This held true even after allowing for smoking, cholesterol and blood pressure.

The deeper finding was why. It was not only money. What separated the ranks was control and autonomy: how much say a person had over their own time, work and life. Too little control causes long-term stress. Over years, that stress turns into heart disease and early death. Marmot later called this the "status syndrome." In 2008 he led the World Health Organization's Commission on Social Determinants of Health (the social conditions that decide health). It called these conditions the "causes of the causes" of illness: who has power, money, security, education and a healthy environment, and so who does well and who does not.

That one idea is why this index exists, and why it is built this way. If control and security shape your health, then sovereignty (control over your own life and money) is not just a money matter. It affects your health. And if advantage builds up over generations, then legacy is not only estate planning. It is passing better life chances to your children. Where you live sets how much each of these forces affects you.

Why these six pillars

The pillars follow the same list of social conditions that health researchers already use, turned into the choices you actually face about where you live. Each pillar is scored 0 to 100 from public data. The columns in the index match these pillars exactly.

Income and Opportunity

How well you can earn and keep wealth: how much you can earn, how much tax lets you keep, how freely banks and investing work, and how far your money goes. Income sits at the bottom of the health ladder. But what matters for wellbeing is the money you keep and can use, not the size of the national economy (GDP).

Sources: UNDP income index (GNI per capita, PPP); Heritage tax burden, financial and investment freedom; World Bank PPP price level.
Education

How much schooling people get, and how good it is, for you and your children. Education is one of the most reliable signs of lifelong health. It is also a main way advantage passes to the next generation.

Source: UNDP education index (expected and mean years of schooling).
Health and Longevity

The bottom line of the whole index: how long and how well people actually live. Life expectancy (how long people live on average) is the best single summary of a country's health.

Source: life expectancy at birth, UNDP Human Development Report 2025.
Environment

The quality of the air, water and nature around you. This is not about looks. Living near green space is linked to lower death rates. It is also linked to a smaller health gap between rich and poor. Air pollution directly causes disease, and the harm can be measured.

Source: Yale Environmental Performance Index 2024 (58 weighted indicators).
Lifestyle and Belonging

Whether you can settle in, fit in and be accepted. Having people around you is one of the strongest signs of a long life. Loneliness raises your risk of dying about as much as obesity does. This pillar measures how welcoming a place is to outsiders and minorities, how easily you can get by in the language, and how safe daily life is.

Sources: Williams Institute Global Acceptance Index; EF English Proficiency Index 2025; UNODC and World Bank intentional-homicide rate.
Legacy and Sovereignty

Whether what you have is safe and can be passed on: how well the law protects what you own, whether courts are fair, whether government is honest and stable, and whether all of it lasts over time. This is the "control over your life" finding from Whitehall, applied to a whole country.

Sources: Heritage property rights, judicial effectiveness, government integrity; World Bank Worldwide Governance Indicators (political stability).

The investor lens: Capital and Enterprise

The six pillars show where a life goes well. Many Sovereo readers also have money that crosses borders, and that is a different question. The Capital and Enterprise lens is a separate score for how a country treats money: how open it is to money from abroad, how freely you can take your money back out, and how easy it is to run a business there. It has its own column in the index and its own filter in the shortlist. We keep it apart from the six-pillar overall score on purpose. What makes a good life and what makes a good place for money are not the same, and you should be able to see them separately.

Capital and Enterprise

Three parts, with the most weight on the one that matters most in a crisis. Foreign Investment measures how open a country is to money coming in from abroad. Capital Repatriation, the exit test, measures whether you can take your money back out without rules trapping it. It counts the most. Business Environment measures how well the law protects what you own and how easy it is to do business there. The combined score runs 0 to 100. A country can score well for living and poorly for money. This lens is built to show exactly that difference.

Sources: Chinn-Ito Index (KAOPEN capital-account openness, 2023 update, a measure of how freely money can move in and out) for repatriation; Heritage Index of Economic Freedom 2026 (investment freedom, business freedom, property rights) for the other two. Where that money-movement data is missing, a country's score is marked provisional.

How the scores are built

We score each country on several factors and combine them. Experts call this Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis. It is the same open, step-by-step scoring that businesses use to pick sites. We put every measure on the same 0 to 100 scale, across all countries, so they can be compared. A pillar score is the average of its measures. The overall Sovereo Index is the average of the six pillars. The Capital and Enterprise lens is scored on its own three parts and shown as a separate column and shortlist filter. It is not added into the six-pillar overall. Countries missing data on more than half the pillars are marked "insufficient data" instead of being ranked on too little evidence.

What this is and is not. The index is an educational ranking built from public data (2026 edition). It is not legal, tax, immigration or financial advice. It cannot reflect your personal situation. That is what the companion Best-Fit Countries tool is for. Data and country programmes change. Figures are correct only at the time they were collected.
