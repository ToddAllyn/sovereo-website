How to read this, in plain English

You do not need to know anything about finance to use this. Here is what the numbers mean.

The percentage: cheaper or more expensive

This is the main answer. If it says a country is "40% cheaper," it means a normal month of life there (rent, food, transport, the usual) costs roughly 40 percent less than where you live now. "More expensive" means the opposite.

The two bars

Each bar shows how pricey a country is compared to the United States, which we set at 1.00 as a fixed yardstick. A bar below 1.00 means cheaper than the US. Above 1.00 means pricier. The longer the bar, the more expensive the country.

Your budget, matched abroad

If you enter what you spend in a month, we show how much you would need to live the same way in the other country. We show it first in that country's own money, then in US dollars so it is easy to picture. If the other country is cheaper, that number is smaller than what you spend now.

Why "purchasing power," not just the exchange rate

An exchange rate only tells you how many units of foreign money you get for a dollar. It does not tell you that a haircut, a bus ride, or a week of groceries is far cheaper in some places. We use World Bank "purchasing power" data, which measures what money actually buys in each country. So the comparison shows real daily life, not just currency math. The exchange rate is used only for the final "in US dollars" figure, and we show you the exact rate we used.

Treat it as a guide, not a quote

These are country-wide averages for orientation. A big capital city costs more than a small town, and your own lifestyle matters. Use this to see the rough size of the difference. Then check the details before you decide.

The data. We measure cost of living as each country's price level, compared with the United States. To get it, we divide the World Bank purchasing power parity (PPP) factor by the market exchange rate. PPP compares money across countries by what it buys locally. The World Bank figures use 2021 as the base year and are adjusted for inflation (rising prices). We fetch live exchange rates where we can. If not, we use a stored set of rates dated September 2026. The tool shows which one it used. Figures are country-level estimates, not city-level prices, and are best estimates rather than exact facts.
