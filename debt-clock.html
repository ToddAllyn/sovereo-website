How the US compares: government debt as a share of the economy

A raw debt total is hard to judge. The number that lets you compare countries fairly is debt-to-GDP: the government's total debt measured against everything the country produces in a year. A country making a lot can safely carry more debt than a country making a little, just as a high earner can carry a larger mortgage. Higher on this list means more strain.

How to read all of this, in plain English

You do not need any background in finance. Here is the whole page, simply.

What is the "national debt"?

It is the total a government has borrowed and not yet repaid. Most years a government spends more than it collects in taxes and borrows to cover the gap. Those gaps pile up over the years, and the pile is the national debt. The US clock at the top is that pile for the United States, updated to the second.

What does "debt-to-GDP" mean, and why use it?

GDP is the value of everything a country produces in a year, its income. Debt-to-GDP compares what the government owes to what the country earns. A ratio of 100% means the debt equals one full year of the whole economy's output. It lets you compare a giant economy and a small one on the same footing, which a raw dollar total cannot.

Is a high ratio automatically a crisis?

No. Japan sits above 200% and remains stable because it borrows in its own currency at low rates from its own savers. What matters is whether a country borrows in money it controls, how much the interest costs each year, and whether lenders stay willing to lend. A high and rising ratio in a country that borrows in someone else's currency is the real warning sign.

The "Borrows in" column, and why it may matter more than the ranking

Next to each country we mark what currency its debt is in, because that decides whether a big number is dangerous. Own currency means the government borrows in money it can print, so it can always repay; the risk shows up as inflation (rising prices) or a weaker currency, not a default (failing to repay). That is why Japan carries more than double the US ratio without a crisis. Euro (shared) means a country that uses the euro. It owes in euros but cannot print them on its own, so its debt acts partly like foreign debt. That is what made Greece's crisis possible. Mixed means a meaningful slice is in foreign currency. Foreign-heavy means the country leans on dollars or euros it cannot issue, or uses the US dollar outright (Panama, Ecuador); these are the ones that actually default when the dollar gets stronger. Read the "Borrows in" tag together with the ratio: a high ratio in an own-currency country is a very different thing from a moderate ratio that is foreign-heavy.

One honest caveat about the ranking

This is "gross" debt, which counts what a government owes without subtracting what it owns. A few countries look far more indebted than they are because they also hold huge offsetting assets. Singapore is the clearest case: the law bars it from spending what it borrows, so it invests the money instead. Once you subtract what it owns (its "net" position), it is in strong shape despite a high gross number. Norway and Hong Kong are similar. Treat the ranking as a first read, not a verdict.

Why Sovereo tracks this

Because government debt is one of the forces behind a weakening currency, higher taxes, and a rising cost of living. Those forces reach your income, your savings, and your retirement whether or not you ever cross a border. Watching the debt is not a reason to panic. It is a way to see where money is likely to hold its value, and which decisions to make before conditions change.

Where these numbers come from. The live US total, its split between debt owed to the public and debt the government owes itself, and the per-second growth rate are pulled from the US Treasury's official "Debt to the Penny" dataset (Fiscal Data API), updated each business day. Between updates, the clock keeps counting at the recent real pace of borrowing. The country comparison is general government gross debt as a percent of GDP from the International Monetary Fund's World Economic Outlook, fetched live from the IMF DataMapper and labelled with the data year; if that feed cannot be reached, the page shows the last confirmed IMF World Economic Outlook (October 2025) figures as a dated reference. The per-US-citizen figure divides the live total by an estimated US population of about 342 million (a 2026 estimate) and is illustrative, since the debt is not actually assigned to individuals. Gross debt does not subtract what governments own, which overstates the burden for a few asset-rich states as noted above. The "Borrows in" classification (own currency, euro, mixed, or foreign-heavy) is a dated editorial judgment for the countries shown, drawn from Bank for International Settlements and International Monetary Fund work on the currency composition of sovereign debt (2025); currency shares shift over time, so treat it as a directional guide, not a precise share. All figures are point in time and provided for general education, not financial advice.
